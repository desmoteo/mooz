How it works

Mooz turns a rehearsal recording into a master. This page says what each part of the app is for, in the order you meet it. Nothing here does anything the mooz command cannot: the app starts that command and shows what it leaves behind.

Projects

A project is a folder of recordings and one settings file, rehearsal.toml. New project asks for the folder, shows the takes it finds there, and writes the settings file into it. A folder that already has one is taken as it is.

A take is one moment recorded. A recorder that writes a file per track (TrMic for its own microphones, Tr2 for an input, TrLR for its own mix-down) gives one take of several tracks: the microphones are the room track, the inputs are close tracks.

Removing a project from the list deletes nothing on disk.

Takes and mastering

  • Tick the takes and the profiles to master them with, then press Remaster. One job runs at a time.
  • A profile is a sound: a few settings on top of the project's. Each profile's masters go to a folder of their own, so a take can be compared across profiles.
  • While a job runs, the bar on top shows the take and the stage it is at, the time so far and an estimate of what is left. Cancel stops it; takes already finished keep their masters.
  • Under the bar, "The pipeline of ..." opens the graph of the take being made. A box at work is orange and fills from the left as far as it is; the line above the graph names what is at work and at what percentage. Done boxes have a full outline, the ones kept from an earlier run a dashed one, and the pale ones are still waiting. Several stems are worked on at the same time, so several boxes can be orange at once. The same marks are on the take's own page, on its pipeline graph, while that take runs.
  • Separate only does the slow part (splitting the takes into stems, on the graphics card) and keeps the stems for later.
  • A/B files too writes, next to each master, a file with the recording and the master in turns at the same loudness.
  • A take already mastered with the same settings, recordings and program is left as it is: its row says "up to date". So a job that stopped half-way can simply be started again, and goes on where it stopped. Tick "Also masters that are up to date" to make those again too.
  • A master made again still starts from the stems the take was separated into before (dashed on the job's graph): they depend on the recording only, and separating is the slow part. Tick "Separate again too" to have the chosen takes separated again as well, several minutes per track; the kept stems are replaced.
  • If a job stops with "the system stopped the run: it needed more memory", a take was too long for the memory the run may use. The limit goes with the memory the machine has free when the job starts: close what you do not need and start it again.

Each row then shows, per profile: the master's loudness, how much of the recording's loudness range it kept, and how much of the time the limiter works (in orange above 5 %: the master is pushed hard). Original, Play and A/B play the recording, the master and the A/B file, and going between them keeps the moment of the song. Mix plays the mix before the master step, which is also written as a file of its own (<take>_mix): nothing is clipped or limited in it, so the drum hits keep all their height, and it is quieter than the master by what the master step takes off them.

A take's page

Press a take's title, or Stages under a profile. The page draws what happened to the take, in two ways, and every file a run left behind can be heard from it. A third tab, Compare blind, puts two profiles' masters side by side (below).

Stage by stage
A grid. Columns are the stages, left to right; rows are the recording and its stems, the drum kit last. A row is one stem's way through the pipeline. A round pad is the stem at that stage; a pad with a word on it is another file at the same point (what the clean-up took out, a drum sample, the A/B file).
Pipeline graph
The same files as boxes and arrows: which goes into which. The close tracks' stems have boxes of their own, with the share each puts into the merged stem on its arrow. A dashed box is a step with no file of its own (a separation: press it for each model's own output) or a file that is not used (bleed on a close track). Press a box and what goes into it and what comes out of it stay lit.

Under the drawing, for the chosen pad or box: what the take's report says the run did at that point (a stem's chain step by step, the levels in the mix, the limiter), and the file's path.

With a take that has close tracks, the Separated column names every pad. merged is what the chain starts from: the close track's stem with a little of the room's. room and Tr2 are what it is made of, each at its level in the merged stem. A dashed Tr2 is not used: the instrument is only bleed on that track.

The tabs with the profiles' names show the same take under another profile. A take not mastered with a profile yet has its recording and, once separated, its stems; Master this take starts a job for it alone. While a job works on the shown take, the stage it is at is marked.

The stages

Recording
The files as recorded, whole. The room track is what the pipeline works on.
Separated
The stems the separation models find in it: voice, guitar, bass, other, and the kit split into kick, snare, toms, hi-hat and cymbals. Close tracks are separated too and what they really hold (voice, bass) is lined up with the room's stem and merged with it.
Cleaned
The kit's stems after the clean-up: snare buzz, bleed between drums, gates. "Taken out" is what was removed.
Stem chain
Each stem through its own chain: tone, dynamics, and samples laid under the drums' hits.
Drum bus
The kit balanced and glued as one instrument.
In the mix
Every part at its level and place, and the untouched recording under them at its share. These add up to the mix.
Mix
After the mix bus: tonal balance, compression, saturation.
Master
After loudness and the limiter: the finished file.

Listening

  • Same moment. Going from one file of a take to another keeps the moment of the song, so a change can be heard on the same bar. Press the playing pad again to pause.
  • Levels lined up. The stage files are written 12 dB under their real level so the loudest drum hits do not clip. With this on, everything on a take's page plays 12 dB down, the master too, so two stages compare by ear. Turn it off to hear each file as it is on disk.
  • Keys. With a pad in focus, ← → go along the stem's row and ↑ ↓ down the stage's column. In the graph, ← → follow the arrows and ↑ ↓ go through a layer. While something plays, the next pad or box takes over.
  • Drum samples are short single hits: they play from their start, on their own.

Comparing blind

Which of two profiles sounds better is for your ears to say, and they say it best without knowing which is which. On a take's page, Compare blind takes two profiles that both have a master of the take.

  • Start reads both masters and plays them in step as A and B, each turned down to the quieter one's loudness. Which profile is A is drawn by lot.
  • A and B switch with no gap, on the same sample of the song. Loop from here and Loop to here repeat a passage: a snare fill, the start of a chorus.
  • Keys: A and B choose the side, X the other one, Space plays and pauses, [ and ] set the loop.
  • The list also holds each profile's mix ("pro, the mix"). A master against its own mix is the master step alone, at the same loudness: on a take's page the mix plays louder than the master, and louder nearly always sounds better.
  • Say which sounds better, or that you hear no difference, with a few words on why if you like. Only then does the page say which was which. Another round draws A and B again; several rounds on several takes say more than one.

Every choice is kept in listening.json next to rehearsal.toml, and the takes' page adds them up per pair of profiles. A verdict is about the two files that were heard: when one of them is mastered again it stays in the list, marked, and is no longer counted. Take back removes one pressed by mistake.

Settings and profiles

  • The form shows the settings changed most often. A dot marks what the project sets itself; Back to default takes the setting out of the file again.
  • The whole file, at the bottom, is rehearsal.toml as text, for everything else: one song's own settings, a stem's chain. Every setting is explained in defaults.toml, whose path the page gives.
  • Every profile can be edited, in the same form and as text. Changing a built-in one keeps your version in the project (presets/<name>.toml); the built-in one is not touched, and Back to the built-in returns to it.
  • Nothing is saved that would not load: a mistake comes back as a message and the file stays as it was. The previous rehearsal.toml is kept as rehearsal.toml.bak.
  • A changed setting is heard after the takes are mastered again. That takes about a third of the time when the change is to the mix or the master: each stem's own chain is kept from the last run and used as it is, unless that stem's settings changed. The job's line says "kept" next to such a stem.

Where the files are

Masters
Next to the recordings, in the profile's folder (processed/ unless a profile or the project says otherwise).
Stage files
In stages/<take>/ inside that folder, about half a gigabyte per take. They line up in a DAW.
Stems
In a cache next to the recordings, shared by every profile, so a take is separated once.
Kept chains
In processed/chains/ next to the recordings, shared by every profile: each stem after its chain, a bit over a gigabyte per take. It can be deleted at any time; the next run makes it again.
Verdicts
In listening.json next to rehearsal.toml: what the blind comparisons said.
Reports
In reports/ inside the profile's folder: the figures as pictures, and the numbers the take's page shows.

Two projects over the same recordings share these folders: a profile run from one overwrites the other's masters of that profile.

The app answers only to this machine, and has no log-in: do not put it on a network.
